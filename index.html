A short history of carrying electricity

The power bank, from a box of AA cells to 140 watts.

1991 – 2008

Borrowed cells

Sony sold the first commercial lithium-ion cell in 1991. Phones of the 1990s and 2000s had small batteries and long standby times, so a spare charge was a luxury. Early "portable chargers" were plastic cases that held disposable AA batteries and a boost circuit with a single plug.

  • Capacity: about 1,000–2,500 mAh in practice
  • Output: 5 V, often well under 1 A
  • Catch: heavy, slow, and the AA cells ran out

2009 – 2014

The 18650 years

Touchscreen smartphones drained in a day. Makers started packing cylindrical 18650 lithium-ion cells (the same size used in laptops) into rechargeable bricks with a USB-A port and a row of LEDs to show charge. Capacities jumped, and the power bank became a common gadget.

  • Capacity: 5,000–10,000 mAh became normal
  • Output: 5 V, 1–2 A over USB-A
  • Catch: cheap packs overstated their mAh

2015 – 2019

Slim, fast, and USB-C

Pouch-style lithium-polymer cells made banks thinner, and aluminum shells replaced shiny plastic. Fast-charging standards such as Qualcomm Quick Charge arrived, and USB-C (finalized in 2014) started replacing micro-USB. Big 20,000 mAh packs could now charge a tablet or a light laptop.

  • Capacity: 10,000 mAh slim, 20,000 mAh large
  • Output: 18 W fast charging
  • Catch: many competing fast-charge protocols

2020 – today

Power Delivery and GaN

USB Power Delivery became the shared language of chargers. Gallium nitride (GaN) parts run cooler than silicon, so one bank can output 65 W to 140 W and charge a laptop. Digital screens show watts in and out, and Qi2 magnets let a bank snap to the back of a phone and charge it wirelessly.

  • Capacity: 10,000–27,000 mAh
  • Output: 30–140 W over USB-C; PD 3.1 allows up to 240 W
  • Catch: airlines cap carry-ons at 100 Wh, and some now restrict use in flight

Next

Beyond lithium: ideas in development

None of this is the mainstream product yet. Researchers and companies are working on cells that hold more energy and are safer, and on bodies that gather a little power themselves.

  • Solid-state cells: a solid electrolyte instead of liquid, aimed at higher density and less fire risk
  • Silicon anodes: more lithium storage per gram than graphite
  • Sodium-ion: cheaper materials, good in cold weather, lower density today
  • Solar skins and wireless coils: top-ups without a cable, though solar adds only a trickle

What is inside

Cells

Lithium-ion or lithium-polymer cells store the energy. Each cell is about 3.7 V, which is why capacity is quoted at 3.7 V.

Boost converter

Raises the cell voltage to the 5 V (or 9, 12, 20 V) that your device asks for. Some energy is lost as heat, so you never get the full rated mAh.

Protection board

Stops overcharge, over-discharge, short circuits, and overheating. A pack with no real protection is the one to avoid.

Control chip

Talks to your device over USB-C PD to agree on voltage and current, and drives the LEDs or display.

How many charges will I get?

Buy and use it safely

Check the Wh

Wh = mAh × 3.7 ÷ 1000. Most airlines allow up to 100 Wh in carry-on bags, never in checked bags. Check your airline's current rules, since some now limit use during flights.

Buy known brands

Look for safety certifications on the label and independent test reviews. Unmarked bargain packs often overstate capacity.

Stop using damaged packs

If a bank swells, gets unusually hot, or smells odd, stop using it and take it to a battery recycling point.

Match the wattage

A laptop needs a bank that supports USB-C PD at the wattage it asks for, plus a cable rated for it.
